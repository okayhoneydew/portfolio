02
The problem

A high-touch service breaks when every inquiry gets treated like a qualified project. The work needed a way to separate curiosity from fit, move serious prospects toward a call, and keep follow-up from depending on whether I remembered to send the next message.

I treated the client journey as an operating system rather than a contact form: application stages, scheduling, qualification, tags, automated follow-up, routing, and human review all had to work together.

System / 02
Lifecycle design

The pipeline was doing work, not just storing names.

CRM stages interacted with scheduling and follow-up. Intake questions helped identify fit before a large time investment. Tags and workflow triggers could launch SMS or email follow-up, while webhooks connected repetitive steps to Zapier or Make. Human judgment stayed in the loop where the project still needed interpretation.

System / 03
Automation trace

Every automated step needed a reason to exist.

The goal was not to automate the relationship. It was to remove the repetitive coordination around it: reminders, follow-up, tagging, routing, and moving information between tools.

When a manual step repeated often enough to become friction, I looked for a trigger and a reliable next action. If the decision required context, it stayed manual.

Operating principle

More leads were not automatically better leads.

When funnel or CRM data showed poor-fit inquiries getting through, I changed the system: intake questions, filtering, ad creative, website copy, buttons and links, and the sales process itself. Recorded calls also became another source of feedback for tightening the framework.

The point was to improve the quality of the path, not just its volume. A good client system should save time on the wrong opportunities while making the right ones easier to progress.
